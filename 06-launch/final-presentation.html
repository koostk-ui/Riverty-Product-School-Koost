Slide 1 · Strategy

The problem, the promise, and the bet

Problem hook

Riverty's users like Lena juggle several BNPL apps and pay at the till with someone else's card, while Klarna and PayPal grow into the stores and checkouts Riverty can't reach, and with a brand preference of only 6%, every month without a card moves Riverty closer to being a replaceable checkout button.

Value proposition

For busy BNPL users like Lena, who want money to leave their account only after they've decided to keep what they bought, the Riverty card lets them pay later anywhere, in stores and online, with one clear view of what's due. Unlike Klarna's debit-first card or PayPal's separate instalment products, it keeps the "decide first, pay after" habit in every setting and sends users back to Riverty's partner merchants instead of away from them.

New value: Riverty moves from a checkout option Lena doesn't prefer to an everyday card she carries. That closes the in-store and heavy-user gaps from the brief and gives Riverty a direct consumer relationship for the first time.

Why now (full text)

Why now: Klarna's card base is growing, and PayPal now offers instalments at Amazon.de and a virtual card for in-store payments, so Lena's habits are being set by competitors. From 20 November 2026, most BNPL falls under consumer credit rules, and Riverty carries that compliance cost with or without a card. Riverty's banking licence and Mastercard platform are already in place, and a consumer card is the next product that can earn that investment. The merchant benefit is a hypothesis, not something the brief establishes, so it should be one of the first things tested in discovery.

Data-backed hypothesis

Based on 15 interviews in which heavy BNPL users said they use Riverty only when nothing else is offered, choose BNPL for returns and security, and fear forgetting to pay, plus 2022 data showing that 52% of Riverty users transacted only once while heavy and hardcore users grew 25 to 30%, I believe that letting "decide first, pay later" work at merchants that don't offer Riverty, with returns and dues in one place, for heavy Riverty app users like Lena will result in her keeping purchases inside Riverty instead of switching to Klarna, PayPal or a debit card, as measured by at least 50% of active pilot cardholders making an off-network card purchase in the final four weeks, with a rising trend. I will protect partner-merchant checkout conversion, on-time payments, and SCHUFA, fee and decline complaints, and will make a go/no-go decision after a 10-week friends-and-family pilot: proceed at 50% or more, extend or pivot between 10% and 49%, and stop below 10% with no upward trend after week 4, unless a fixable fault explains the result.

Slide 2 · Research

Lena's workaround, and where it breaks

Competitive analysis / workaround: external tools she relies on

ObservedPayPal: the preferred online method for most interviewees, valued for its 30-day deferral and as a peer-to-peer tool.

ObservedKlarna (app and invoice): used for reminders, transparency, and its return handling, where payment is paused until the return is confirmed.

ObservedDebit card, bank app or phone wallet: the direct-payment fallback.

ObservedBudget book or budget apps (for example Monkey, finanzguru): used by several interviewees to track spending, though one said categorising by hand was "too much work."

InferredCalendar, notes or reminders for due dates and return windows: not mentioned in the research.

Friction (moment of misery)

She is forced to abandon "decide first, pay later" whenever Riverty isn't offered, either switching to Klarna or PayPal or paying upfront by debit card and waiting for a refund. Meanwhile her open payments sit across several apps with different due dates and return rules, so she risks losing track and forgetting to pay.

Core frustration

Lena can't count on "decide first, pay later" being available when she needs it. Her protection is tied to whichever provider the merchant happens to show, so she has to re-negotiate how she pays on every purchase, and she is never sure where her money, her return and her due date stand.

PRD highlights: must-haves for the pay-after-you-keep invoice

One invoice per cleared card purchase, in Riverty.

No debit at purchase. Her bank account is charged only when she pays the invoice.

Fixed due date set at creation. Risk and Finance sign off, not user-selectable.

Real-time approval at authorisation. No approval means no purchase and no invoice.

Invoice from the final cleared amount.

Invoice detail screen with a Pay action.

No interest or fee if paid by the due date.

Slide 4 · Validation

The experiment plan

Hypothesis (expected outcome)

Offering the Riverty Card to eligible heavy users will raise net combined Riverty volume per eligible user by about 2% (EUR 3.60) over 28 days, because card purchases at merchants outside the Riverty network replace spend that would otherwise go to Klarna, PayPal or debit.

Control (A)

Eligible, fully onboarded heavy app users in the holdout get no card offer and no card messaging.

Full text

Eligible, fully onboarded heavy app users in the holdout get no card offer and no card messaging. They keep today's Riverty experience: Riverty checkout at partner merchants, and the existing return notifications (consumer and merchant), return pause, keep-or-return nudges, push notifications and autopay on their checkout invoices. Where Riverty isn't offered, Lena still switches to Klarna or PayPal, or pays directly by debit card and waits for refunds on returns. Her open payments still sit across several apps with different due dates and return rules, so the moment of misery is unchanged. Measured: net combined Riverty volume (checkout only), checkout completion rate, and on-time payment rate. Card measures are empty by design.

Variant (B)

The one change: eligible, fully onboarded heavy app users in the offered arm get the Riverty Card, whose only mode is the pay-after-you-keep invoice.

Full text

The one change: eligible, fully onboarded heavy app users in the offered arm get the Riverty Card, whose only mode is the pay-after-you-keep invoice. The offer, card, wallet setup and invoice ship as one unit. Card invoices use the same existing return notifications, pause, nudges, push notifications and autopay that checkout invoices already have, with no new feature built for the card. Lena can pay with the card at merchants outside the Riverty network, so nothing leaves her account at purchase. She gets one invoice per purchase, due in 14 days (placeholder), and can inspect the item first. If she reports a return, the due date pauses as it does today. Her reminders and nudges arrive as they do today, and she can set autopay for small amounts. Everything else is identical to control. Measured: the same as control, plus activation, off-network share, and the displacement question for activated users.

Primary metric

Net combined Riverty volume per eligible user over 28 days: checkout volume plus card volume, minus refunds, for every eligible user in both arms.

Full text

Cap extreme buyers at a high percentile, and adjust for each user's pre-test spend to cut noise. It exists in the control arm, it counts only money Riverty keeps, and it rises only if Lena stops sending purchases to Klarna, PayPal or her debit card. Moving a purchase from Riverty checkout to the card doesn't change it.

Slide 5 · Launch

Go-to-market and success metrics

Goal
Conversion

Specifically, activation: an eligible heavy user takes up the card and makes a first purchase outside the Riverty network.

Audience

Lena, the Decide-First Juggler: a salaried, phone-first online shopper who spreads BNPL across Klarna, PayPal and Riverty, and often orders items she may send back.

Launch tier
M, Targeted

Focused comms to the eligible heavy users, through owned channels only. No press, no events, no paid media.

Channels Owned: Action center (primary) Owned: Push notification (the trigger) Owned: Email (the explainer)
Success metrics dashboard

1. Activation rate

primary, read at day 14

Share of offered, eligible users who activate the card.

Target: at least 5% = on track; 2% to under 5% = extend; under 2% = stop signal (together with metric 2).

2. Off-network first purchase rate

read at day 42

Share of activated users who make at least one purchase at a merchant outside the Riverty network within 28 days of activation.

Target: at least 50% = on track; 10% to 49% = extend; under 10% with no upward trend = stop signal.

3. Net combined Riverty volume per eligible user

business outcome, read at day 56

Checkout volume plus card volume, minus refunds, per eligible user over 28 days, offered vs. holdout (baseline EUR 180).

Target: about +2% (EUR 3.60), positive with the 90% interval excluding zero.

Bad signal to watch for

Activation looks fine, but the card isn't used where Riverty wasn't offered. What it looks like: activation is at or above the 5% target, but under 10% of activated users make an off-network purchase, or the displacement answers show most purchases would have stayed with Riverty or never happened.

Likely post-launch decision

Iterate: activation 2 to under 5%, or off-network use 10 to 49%, or displacement below 60%, or a positive but non-significant volume effect, with guardrails holding.

Slide 6 · Story

What I learned, and what I'd do next

Friction points

Biggest point of friction for the course was that the case study did not fit well with the target exercises.

Aha moment

Besides that the content was interesting and the biggest AHA moment was to really always ask myself what data I need to make a decision here.

Key takeaways / next

I would try to use the exercises we did here on my own product area with things we are working on today. Especially rapidly going through the PRD exercises and prototyping.

As to the case study we were working on, I would recommend just launching a riverty card linked with 14 day invoice via Riverty App to selected heavy users and starting to iterate. We have the capabiltities developed now for B2B.
